\begin{abstract}
Verifying that workers wear the required personal protective equipment (PPE)
before entering a work area is a routine but error-prone safety task. Existing
research focuses mostly on the detection model, while the surrounding
workflow---identifying the worker, applying site-specific rules, recording the
result, and producing audit evidence---receives little attention. This paper
presents GearGuard, a cross-platform mobile application that covers that
workflow end to end. We contribute (i) a formal definition of per-scan and
per-worker compliance that is independent of any particular detector, (ii) a
detector-agnostic architecture in which a single interface isolates the machine
learning model from the user interface, storage and reporting layers, and
(iii) an open-source reference implementation in Flutter with scan, attendance,
ranking, archive and PDF-report features.
A smoke test of the author's YOLO model detected the expected equipment class in 11 of 11 validation photos (about 80\,ms per frame on a desktop CPU), while robustness and negative-image experiments expose weaknesses to occlusion (5/11 with a 25\% patch) and to high-visibility colours (false \emph{Vest} on plain orange). The suite has 38 automated tests (75\% line coverage, 20 accessibility checks). A usability study remains future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Construction, manufacturing and logistics sites require workers to wear
helmets, high-visibility vests, gloves and safety footwear. Compliance is
usually checked by a supervisor at the gate, which is slow, inconsistent and
leaves no structured record. Vision-based PPE detection has been studied
extensively~\cite{wang2021chv,karlsson2022,ordrick2025}, building on real-time
single-stage detectors such as the YOLO
family~\cite{wang2023yolov7,jocher2023yolov8}. However, a detector alone does not make a usable safety
tool: supervisors also need to know \emph{who} was scanned, \emph{which} rules
applied, and to retrieve the history for audits.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A detector-independent formalisation of compliance (Section~III).
  \item A layered architecture that treats the detector as a replaceable
        component behind one interface (Section~IV).
  \item An open-source implementation and its verification (Sections~V--VI).
\end{enumerate}
Prior work reports detection accuracy on site imagery
\cite{wang2021chv,karlsson2022,ordrick2025}; the surrounding workflow (worker
identity, site rules, records, audit reports) is rarely studied.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{PPE detection.} Wang et al.~\cite{wang2021chv} released the CHV dataset
(1{,}330 construction-site images) and compared eight YOLO-based detectors.
Karlsson et al.~\cite{karlsson2022} built a YOLOv4 system for five equipment
categories at entry-control points, the scenario closest to ours.
Ordrick et al.~\cite{ordrick2025} trained YOLOv11s on 589 images (1{,}425
instances, seven categories) and reported 94.0\% precision, 90.1\% recall and
92.8\% mAP@50, noting a small validation set and difficulty detecting safety
footwear.

\textbf{Detector families.} Single-stage detectors such as
YOLOv7~\cite{wang2023yolov7} and YOLOv8~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} are the usual
choice for on-device inference.

\textbf{Gap.} These works evaluate the model; they do not specify the
application layer that turns detections into decisions and records. GearGuard
formalises that layer (Section~III) and isolates the model behind one interface
(Section~IV-B).

\section{Problem Formulation}
Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of supported PPE item types (helmet, vest, gloves,
boots, goggles, mask). A site configuration fixes a required subset
$R \subseteq \mathcal{P}$, $R \neq \emptyset$, and a confidence threshold
$\tau \in [0,1]$. For a frame, a detector returns a confidence
$c_p \in [0,1]$ for every $p \in \mathcal{P}$. An item is \emph{detected} when
$c_p \ge \tau$. The set of missing items is
\begin{equation}
M = \{\, p \in R \mid c_p < \tau \,\}.
\end{equation}
A scan is \emph{compliant} iff $M=\emptyset$, and its \emph{score} is
\begin{equation}
s = \frac{|R| - |M|}{|R|}.
\end{equation}
For a worker $w$ with scan set $S_w$, the compliance rate is
\begin{equation}
C_w = \frac{|\{\, x \in S_w \mid M_x=\emptyset \,\}|}{|S_w|},
\end{equation}
and workers are ranked by $C_w$ in descending order, breaking ties by the mean
score $\bar{s}_w$. The daily site rate is the same ratio taken over all scans
recorded on that day. $R$ and $\tau$ are stored with each scan, so historical
results remain valid when the site rules change later.

\section{System Design}
\subsection{Layered Architecture}
GearGuard is organised in four layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}): \emph{domain}
(pure Dart value types: \texttt{PpeItem}, \texttt{Employee},
\texttt{ScanRecord}), \emph{data} (detector, storage, PDF builder),
\emph{state} (\texttt{flutter\_bloc} cubits with immutable states) and
\emph{presentation} (feature modules). Derived quantities such as $C_w$,
rankings and most-missed equipment are computed in the state layer rather than
in widgets, which keeps them unit-testable.

\subsection{Detector Interface}
The only contract between the model and the rest of the app is:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java]
abstract interface class PpeDetector {
  Future<Map<PpeItem, double>> detect();
}
\end{lstlisting}
It is injected at the application root, so replacing the simulated detector
with an on-device model (e.g., a YOLO network invoked through a platform
channel) requires no change to any screen, cubit or report.

\subsection{Interaction Design}
The bottom navigation exposes the five daily tasks (Home, Attendance, Scan,
Team, Archive). Design decisions include: violations sorted first in the
attendance list; a one-tap ``Scan'' action for workers who have not yet checked
in; a per-item confidence checklist with bounding-box overlay on the scan
result (Fig.~\ref{fig:scan}); and status that is never conveyed by colour alone
(every status pill carries an icon and text). Light and dark themes are
provided.

\subsection{Persistence and Reporting}
Employees and scans are stored as versioned JSON in platform key--value
storage, which works on mobile, desktop and web. A report generator produces a
branded A4 PDF containing summary indicators and a per-scan table for a single
scan, one worker, or the current archive filter.

\section{Implementation and Verification}
The implementation targets Flutter 3.41 / Dart 3.11 and comprises 32 source
files (about 4.9k lines) and Android, iOS, web, Windows and macOS runners.
\subsection{Static Analysis and Tests}
The project passes \texttt{flutter analyze} with no errors or warnings under
the \texttt{very\_good\_analysis} rule set (two informational lints remain).
The test suite has 38 automated tests, detailed in Section~VI-F.
\subsection{Detector Used in the Current Build}
\textbf{The current build uses a simulated detector} that draws item
confidences at random after a short delay. It exists to exercise the complete
workflow without a camera or trained model; consequently \emph{no detection
accuracy is claimed in this paper}.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Preliminary Detector Smoke Test}
We ran the author's TFLite model (6.2\,MB; input $1{\times}640{\times}640{\times}3$,
output $1{\times}10{\times}8400$) on the 11 validation photographs of the
prototype repository: five helmet, three vest and three safety-shoe images, one
object each. Labels are \emph{weak}---the expected class is taken from the file
name and there are no ground-truth boxes, so mAP cannot be computed. With the
app's settings (confidence 0.25, class-wise NMS IoU 0.45) the expected class was
detected in \textbf{11/11} images with both the app's direct $640{\times}640$
resize and standard letterbox preprocessing (Table~\ref{tab:smoke}). Safety-shoe
confidence was markedly lower under direct resize (0.52--0.82) than under
letterbox (0.74--0.92), so letterbox is recommended. Mean latency was 79.7\,ms per
frame (median 79.5, p95 82.1; 30 timed runs after 3 warm-ups; LiteRT, 4 threads)
on a desktop AMD Ryzen CPU (Family 25, Model 80) under Windows.

Table~\ref{tab:tau} counts the photos (of 11) that still yield the expected class

the app's direct resize drops to 9/11 at $\tau=0.6$; at $\tau=0.9$ recall
collapses (5/11 and 3/11). Strict sites must weigh missed equipment against

\subsection{Robustness to Perturbations}
Each photo was perturbed (seeded; letterbox, $\tau=0.25$) and re-scored
(Table~\ref{tab:pert}). The detector is insensitive to darkening, JPEG
compression, $\pm15^\circ$ rotation, sensor noise and $4\times$ down-scaling
(11/11). Strong over-exposure (10/11), heavy blur (8/11) and, above all, a random
grey patch covering 25\% of the image (5/11, mean confidence 0.30) degrade it.
Occlusion is the most relevant real-site failure and is not modelled by the
single-frame rule of Section~III.

\subsection{Negative Images}
On 15 non-PPE images (two natural photographs, six solid colours, six noise
images, one gradient) the model produced 2 detections at $\tau=0.25$ and 1 at
$\tau=0.5$ and 0.7. Both were \emph{Vest} on a plain colour patch: orange-red
(RGB 212, 57, 14) at 0.81 and pink (240, 159, 174) at 0.39; the natural photographs
produced none. This indicates a colour bias toward high-visibility hues that a
threshold alone cannot remove; hard-negative data or colour augmentation is
advisable. The set is small and synthetic and shows a failure mode, not a
false-positive rate.

\subsection{Software Verification}
The suite has 38 automated tests (unit, state, PDF generation, persistence and
end-to-end widget tests) with \textbf{75.1\%} line coverage (1{,}532 of 2{,}040
lines; domain 100\%, data 95\%, core 92\%, state 80\%, feature screens 67\%).
Twenty accessibility checks (Flutter's tap-target, labelled-target and WCAG
text-contrast guidelines on five screens $\times$ light/dark) pass. They
initially \emph{failed} on three light-theme screens, with contrast as low as
2.13:1 (orange text on tinted orange surfaces); darkening the light primary from
\#E85D00 to \#A83600 (6.1:1) and deriving status-text shades algorithmically fixed
all of them. A release web build has a 3.2\,MB main script.

\subsection{Usability Study (Not Conducted)}
No user study has been run, so no usability claim is made. A protocol, a bilingual
System Usability Scale questionnaire and a scoring script are provided in
\texttt{docs/paper/study}; results should be read against the benchmark mean of
68 (SD 12.5)~\cite{hyzy2022sus}. Automated accessibility checks are not a
substitute for testing with supervisors.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Compliance is decided per item from a single frame; occlusion, lighting and
multiple workers per frame are not modelled. Worker identity is chosen from a
list rather than recognised automatically. Storage is local to the device.
Camera-based monitoring of people raises privacy and labour-consent issues; a
deployment must define retention and consent policies and should store scores
rather than images. In Indonesia, processing images of identifiable workers falls under the Personal Data Protection Law~\cite{uupdp2022}, which requires a lawful basis and purpose limitation.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented GearGuard, a detector-agnostic architecture and open-source
implementation of the PPE compliance workflow. Future work is a real on-device
detector behind the same interface, a field usability study, and
department-level reporting.
